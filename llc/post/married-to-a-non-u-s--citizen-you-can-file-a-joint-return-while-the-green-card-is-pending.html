Married to a non-U.S. citizen? You can file a joint return while the green card is pending

You are a U.S. citizen. Your wife or husband is from the Philippines, or any other country, and the green card case is still pending. There is no Social Security number yet. Many couples think they must file "married filing separately" or wait. They do not have to.

The rule

The IRS says that when one spouse is a U.S. citizen or resident and the other is not, "you can choose to treat the nonresident spouse as a U.S. resident for tax purposes." That choice lets you file one joint return.

No Social Security number? Use an ITIN

For a joint return, the IRS says "your spouse must have either a Social Security Number (SSN) or an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN)." The ITIN application, Form W-7, has a box for exactly this case: a spouse who "is electing to file a U.S. federal tax return jointly with a spouse who is a U.S. citizen or resident alien." Form W-7 goes to the IRS together with the joint return.

One exception. The IRS says: "Don't complete Form W-7 if you have an SSN or if you're eligible to get an SSN." If your spouse already has a work permit, they can usually get a Social Security number, and you use that instead.

Living in the U.S. or abroad: both work

  • Your spouse is still abroad, waiting for the visa. The rule looks at who you are married to on the last day of the year, not where your spouse lives. You can still file jointly.
  • You both live abroad. Same answer. A U.S. citizen files a U.S. return from anywhere, and it can be a joint one.
  • Your spouse is already in the U.S. Your spouse may already count as a tax resident by days of presence. If that started partway through the year, IRS Publication 519 has a second choice that treats your spouse as a resident for the whole year, and "you and your spouse must file a joint return for the year of the choice."

How the choice is made

  1. Publication 519 says to "check the box in the Filing Status section of Form 1040 or 1040-SR."
  2. "Attach a statement, signed by both spouses, to your joint return for the first tax year for which the choice applies."
  3. If an ITIN is needed, Form W-7 and the passport verification go in with the return, on paper.

After the first year, the IRS says "you and your spouse can file joint or separate returns in later years."

What to weigh before you choose

  • Worldwide income. "Each spouse must report their entire worldwide income for the year you make the choice." If your spouse earns money abroad, it goes on the U.S. return. The foreign earned income exclusion or the foreign tax credit can lower the tax on it.
  • Joint is usually cheaper. For 2026 the standard deduction is $32,200 on a joint return and $16,100 on a separate one.
  • Not every credit opens up. Some credits, such as the Earned Income Credit, need Social Security numbers.
  • It is a one-time choice. If the choice is ended, the IRS says "neither spouse can make this choice in any later tax year."
  • Without the choice. You file as married filing separately, or as head of household if you support a child or other qualifying relative.
  • Immigration. The IRS says ITINs are "for federal income tax purposes only." An ITIN does not change immigration status.
