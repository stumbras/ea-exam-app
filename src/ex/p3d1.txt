391|Two rules that are easy to mix up. Section 10.29 is about conflicts between clients: you may continue only with each client's informed consent in writing. Section 10.21 is about a client's past mistake: you must tell the client about it and what could happen, but you do not correct it yourself or report it to the IRS.
392|Circular 230 punishes serious misconduct, not every mistake. A sanction generally requires that the practitioner acted on purpose, recklessly, or with gross incompetence. An honest, ordinary error is not enough under Circular 230, though it can still bring a preparer penalty under the tax code.
528|Circular 230 is the set of Treasury rules for people who represent taxpayers before the IRS. It says who may practice, what duties they have, and how they can be disciplined. The Office of Professional Responsibility enforces it.
529|Practice before the IRS means acting for a client in dealings with the IRS: preparing and filing documents, writing or speaking to the IRS, giving written tax advice, and representing the client at meetings and hearings. Simply preparing a tax return is not, by itself, practice.
530|Three groups have unlimited rights: attorneys, CPAs and enrolled agents. They can represent any taxpayer on any matter at any IRS office. Enrolled actuaries and enrolled retirement plan agents are limited to their fields. Unenrolled preparers with the filing season record have only limited rights.
531|An unenrolled preparer can represent a client only if they hold an Annual Filing Season Program record, and only for a return they prepared and signed, and only during an examination or with customer service or the Taxpayer Advocate. They cannot handle appeals or collection matters.
532|Some people can deal with the IRS without being a practitioner because of their special relationship: yourself, an immediate family member, an employer represented by an officer or full-time employee, a partnership by a general partner, and an estate or trust by its fiduciary.
533|To become an enrolled agent by exam: get a PTIN, pass all 3 parts of the Special Enrollment Examination within 3 years, apply on Form 23 within 1 year of passing the last part, and pass a check of your own tax compliance and background. Former IRS employees can qualify through experience.
534|A passing score on one part does not last forever. Each part stays valid for 3 years from the date you passed it. If you do not pass the other parts within that time, the oldest pass expires and that part must be retaken.
535|The exam is normally offered from May 1 through the end of February and is closed in March and April for updating. The 2026–27 window started late, on July 1, 2026, and ends February 28, 2027. Within one testing window you can take each part up to 4 times.
536|Enrolled agents must complete 72 hours of continuing education in each 3-year cycle. At least 16 hours must be done each year, and 2 of those hours must be ethics every year. The courses must come from IRS-approved providers.
537|Enrollment is renewed every 3 years on Form 8554. Which year you renew depends on the last digit of your Social Security number. Separately, the PTIN must be renewed every year.
538|Someone who becomes an enrolled agent in the middle of a 3-year cycle does not need the full 72 hours. The requirement is 2 hours for each month enrolled in that cycle, including 2 hours of ethics each year.
539|Keep proof of continuing education, such as certificates and course details, for 4 years after the date of the renewal for which the hours were used.
540|An enrolled agent who does not renew, or does not finish the required education, is moved to inactive status and may not practice or use the title. If the problem is not fixed, enrollment is terminated.
541|When the IRS makes a proper request for records or information, the practitioner must provide them promptly, unless there is a good-faith belief that they are privileged. If the practitioner does not have them, they must tell the IRS who they believe does. They need not question the client beyond a reasonable inquiry.
542|A practitioner must use due diligence: prepare returns and documents carefully and make sure what is told to the IRS and to clients is correct. Relying on another person's work is acceptable if reasonable care was used in hiring, supervising and reviewing that person.
543|A practitioner may not drag out a case. Unreasonably delaying a matter before the IRS, for example by ignoring requests or missing appointments on purpose, violates Circular 230.
544|A practitioner may not knowingly work with someone who has been disbarred or suspended from practice on IRS matters. They also may not accept help from a former government employee where that would break the rules on former employees.
545|A practitioner who is also a notary may not act as notary on a document in a matter where they represent the client or have a personal interest.
546|A practitioner may not charge a fee that is unconscionable, meaning grossly excessive. Fees that depend on the result are generally banned, with three exceptions connected to IRS examinations, certain refund claims, and court proceedings.
547|Records the client needs to meet their tax obligations must be returned promptly when asked, even if the client has not paid. The practitioner may keep copies. Work the practitioner prepared may be held back for unpaid fees only if state law allows, and even then the client must be given access to review and copy it.
548|A conflict exists when helping one client would hurt another, or when the practitioner's own interests get in the way. Representation may continue only if the practitioner reasonably believes they can serve each client well, the law permits it, and each client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing within 30 days. The consents are kept 36 months.
549|An enrolled agent may describe the credential with phrases such as enrolled to represent taxpayers before the Internal Revenue Service, or enrolled to practice before the IRS. The descriptions must be accurate and not suggest something more.
550|Advertising must be truthful. An enrolled agent may not use the word certified, may not suggest they work for or are endorsed by the IRS, and may not use the IRS logo. Uninvited contact is not allowed if it is false, misleading or involves pressure.
551|A practitioner may advertise fees: fixed fees for routine services, hourly rates, a range of fees, or the fee for a first meeting. Whatever was published must be honored for at least 30 days after the last time it appeared.
552|A copy of every advertisement or solicitation, or for direct mail a list of who received it, must be kept for 36 months from its last use. The IRS can ask to see it.
553|A practitioner must never cash, endorse or deposit a client's federal tax refund check, and may not have a refund sent to an account they own or control. Taking the fee out of a refund this way is prohibited.
554|Being an enrolled agent allows practice before the IRS only. It does not allow giving general legal services such as drafting wills or representing clients in court, which belong to attorneys.
555|Best practices are goals, not rules that are punished. They include being clear with the client about the work, getting the facts right, explaining the consequences of the advice, and dealing fairly with the IRS. People who run a firm should set up procedures that follow them.
556|A practitioner may not sign a return, or advise a position, that lacks a reasonable basis or is an unreasonable position, when acting willfully, recklessly or with gross incompetence. The practitioner must also tell the client about penalties likely to apply and how disclosure can avoid them.
557|A practitioner may generally accept what the client says without checking documents. But they cannot ignore what the information suggests. If something looks wrong, inconsistent or incomplete, they must ask reasonable questions.
558|A practitioner must have the knowledge and skill the matter needs. If they do not, they can become competent by studying the law or by consulting someone who knows the area.
559|The people in charge of a firm's tax practice must take reasonable steps so that everyone in the firm follows Circular 230. If they fail to set up or follow proper procedures and violations happen, they themselves can be sanctioned.
560|Written tax advice must rest on reasonable facts and assumptions and consider everything relevant. The practitioner may not rely on statements they know or should know are wrong, and may not base the advice on the chance that the return will not be audited.
561|There are four sanctions. Censure is a public reprimand. Suspension removes the right to practice for a set time. Disbarment removes it, and reinstatement can be requested only after 5 years. A money penalty can be added, up to the gross income from the misconduct.
562|Disreputable conduct includes being convicted of a tax crime or a crime of dishonesty, giving false information to the IRS, willfully not filing your own tax returns, misusing client funds, and improper solicitation. A practitioner's own tax compliance is part of fitness to practice.
563|A practitioner can be sanctioned for violating the Circular 230 duties willfully, recklessly or through gross incompetence. Recklessness means an extreme departure from ordinary care.
564|A discipline case starts when the Office of Professional Responsibility files a complaint. The practitioner has 30 days to answer. An Administrative Law Judge holds a hearing and decides. Either side can appeal within 30 days to the Treasury Appellate Authority.
565|Some cases need no full hearing first. A practitioner who lost a professional license for cause, was convicted of certain crimes, or was sanctioned by a court in a tax matter can be suspended quickly, and stays suspended until they show the reason no longer applies.
566|A preparer is penalized when a return understates tax because of a position that was unreasonable. The penalty is the greater of $1,000 or 50% of the fee earned for that return. It is avoided if the position had substantial authority, or had a reasonable basis and was disclosed.
567|If the understatement comes from a willful attempt to understate tax or reckless disregard of the rules, the penalty is heavier: the greater of $5,000 or 75% of the fee earned. Any penalty for the same return under the lighter rule is subtracted.
568|These are penalties for breaking the routine duties of a paid preparer: not giving the client a copy, not signing the return, not putting the PTIN on it, not keeping a copy or a list of clients. Each failure has a set dollar penalty. Cashing a client's refund check is penalized here too.
569|For certain credits and Head of Household status, a paid preparer must do four things: ask enough questions to know the client qualifies, complete Form 8867, compute the credit on worksheets, and keep the records for 3 years. A penalty applies for each failure on each return.
570|To prove due diligence the preparer keeps Form 8867, the worksheets used, copies of any client documents relied on, and notes of who gave the information, when, and what questions were asked and answered. These are kept for 3 years.
571|A paid preparer must keep, for 3 years after the end of the return period, either a copy of every return prepared or a list with the taxpayers' names and ID numbers. The return period runs from July 1 to June 30.
572|A preparer may not disclose or use a client's tax return information for anything other than preparing the return, unless the client consents or an exception applies. Doing so knowingly is a crime, and there is also a civil penalty of $250 for each improper disclosure or use.
573|A client's consent to use or disclose return information must be in its own document, signed and dated before the use or disclosure, and must say exactly what is allowed. It lasts 1 year unless it states another period.
574|Communications with an enrolled agent about tax advice get a confidentiality protection like the attorney-client privilege. It is narrow: only noncriminal tax matters before the IRS or in federal court, and it does not cover preparing a return or promoting tax shelters.
575|Two penalties aimed at people who help others cheat. Section 6700 punishes promoting abusive tax shelters. Section 6701 punishes knowingly helping to prepare a document that understates someone's tax: $1,000 per document, or $10,000 if it concerns a corporation's tax.
576|Besides money penalties, the government can ask a federal court for an order that stops a person from acting: section 7407 against a return preparer who keeps breaking the rules, and section 7408 against promoters of abusive tax schemes.
577|Tax evasion is the most serious tax crime: a willful attempt to evade or defeat a tax, with some affirmative act such as hiding income. It is a felony with up to 5 years in prison and a fine up to $100,000 for an individual.
578|Willfully not filing a return, not paying tax, or not keeping required records is a misdemeanor: up to 1 year in prison and a fine up to $25,000 for each year.
579|Signing a return or document that you know is false in an important way, under penalties of perjury, is a felony with up to 3 years in prison. It also covers a preparer who helps prepare a false return.
580|To decide whether a position has substantial authority, only certain sources count: the tax code, regulations, court decisions, IRS revenue rulings and procedures, treaties, committee reports and similar official material. Textbooks, articles and opinions of tax professionals do not count.
581|How strong a position is decides what must be done. With substantial authority, no disclosure is needed. With only a reasonable basis, the position must be disclosed on Form 8275 to avoid penalties. For tax shelters the position must be more likely than not correct.
582|The accuracy-related penalty is 20% of the tax underpaid because of negligence, a substantial understatement, or a serious valuation error. It doubles to 40% for a gross valuation misstatement or an undisclosed foreign financial asset.
583|An understatement is substantial for an individual when it is more than the greater of 10% of the correct tax or $5,000. Corporations have their own test. Example: correct tax $30,000, reported $26,000: the $4,000 understatement is below $5,000, so not substantial.
584|When part of an underpayment is due to fraud, the penalty is 75% of that part. The IRS has the burden of proving fraud by clear and convincing evidence, a higher standard than in ordinary civil cases. There is no time limit for assessing tax on a fraudulent return.
585|Filing a return that is obviously wrong on its face and based on a frivolous argument, such as wages are not income, brings a $5,000 penalty. The same applies to frivolous requests for hearings, offers or payment plans, though the IRS first gives 30 days to withdraw them.
586|Claiming a refund or credit larger than allowed, without reasonable cause, costs 20% of the excessive amount. It discourages inflated refund claims even when no tax is underpaid.
587|The Office of Professional Responsibility is the part of the IRS that interprets and enforces Circular 230. It investigates practitioner misconduct and brings disciplinary cases.
588|The Return Preparer Office handles registration matters: issuing PTINs, enrolling agents, overseeing the enrolled agent exam and continuing education providers, and running the Annual Filing Season Program.
589|The Annual Filing Season Program is a voluntary program for preparers who are not attorneys, CPAs or enrolled agents. They complete 18 hours of education each year, including a refresher course with a test, and receive a Record of Completion and limited representation rights.
947|Form 23 is the application to become an enrolled agent, filed after passing the exam. A fee is paid and the IRS checks the applicant's tax compliance and background.
948|Enrolled agents file Form 8554 every 3 years to renew their enrollment and report that they completed their continuing education.
949|Form W-12 is used to apply for or renew a PTIN, though most people do it online. Every paid preparer needs a current PTIN.
950|Form 8867 is the due diligence checklist a paid preparer must complete and submit with any return claiming the earned income credit, the child tax credit group, the American Opportunity credit, or Head of Household status.
951|Form 8275 tells the IRS about a position on a return that might be questioned. Disclosing a position that has a reasonable basis protects the taxpayer from the accuracy penalty and the preparer from the preparer penalty.
952|Form 8275-R is the disclosure used when a return position goes against a Treasury regulation. The position must be taken in good faith and have a reasonable basis.
953|A taxpayer or anyone else can use Form 14157 to report a tax return preparer to the IRS for misconduct, such as filing a return without consent or changing it to inflate a refund.
982|The Office of Professional Responsibility enforces Circular 230. It has authority over practitioner conduct and can seek censure, suspension, disbarment or money penalties.
983|The Return Preparer Office manages who can prepare returns and practice: PTINs, enrolled agent enrollment and renewal, the Special Enrollment Examination, and the Annual Filing Season Program.
984|The Special Enrollment Examination is the test to become an enrolled agent. It has 3 parts: Individuals, Businesses, and Representation, Practices and Procedures. Each part has 100 multiple-choice questions.
985|The Annual Filing Season Program gives unenrolled preparers a yearly Record of Completion after 18 hours of education. Holders are listed in the IRS directory of preparers and get limited rights to represent clients whose returns they prepared.
986|An Administrative Law Judge is the independent official who conducts the hearing in a Circular 230 discipline case and issues the decision, which can be appealed to the Treasury Appellate Authority.
987|Censure is the mildest sanction: a public reprimand. The practitioner may continue to practice, but the censure is published, and conditions may be attached for a period.
988|A suspended practitioner cannot practice before the IRS for the period of the suspension. They may still prepare returns, but may not represent clients or call themselves eligible to practice.
989|Disbarment takes away the right to practice before the IRS. A disbarred practitioner can ask for reinstatement only after 5 years, and the IRS decides whether to allow it.
990|In Circular 230, practitioner means someone authorized to practice before the IRS: an attorney, CPA, enrolled agent, enrolled actuary or enrolled retirement plan agent. The duties and sanctions of Circular 230 apply to them.
991|Due diligence means doing the work with reasonable care: asking the right questions, not ignoring warning signs, applying the law correctly, and checking the work before it goes to the client or the IRS.
992|Substantial authority means the support for a position is strong when weighed against the authorities that go the other way. It is less than more likely than not but more than a reasonable basis. With it, no disclosure is needed to avoid the penalty.
993|Reasonable basis is a fairly low standard, but higher than merely arguable or not frivolous. The position must be based on at least one real authority. It protects against penalties only if the position is disclosed on the return.
994|More likely than not means better than even odds that the position would be upheld if challenged. It is the level required for tax shelter positions and reportable transactions.
995|A contingent fee depends on the outcome, for example 30% of the refund obtained. Circular 230 bans it for preparing an original return, because it encourages aggressive positions, and allows it only in a few situations involving IRS challenges and court cases.
996|A conflict of interest exists when representing one client would directly work against another client, or when there is a real risk the representation will be limited by duties to someone else or by the practitioner's own interests. A common example is representing both spouses in a dispute.
997|A frivolous position is one that is plainly wrong, with no serious support in the law. Taking one can lead to penalties for the taxpayer and sanctions for the practitioner.
