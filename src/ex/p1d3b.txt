257|The credit for the elderly or disabled disappears at very low income levels, and nontaxable Social Security reduces it further. So although many people are old enough, very few actually receive it.
684|Schedule 1 has two parts. Part I adds income that has no line on Form 1040, such as business income, unemployment and rental income. Part II lists adjustments that are subtracted to reach AGI, such as the IRA deduction and student loan interest.
685|Schedule 1-A is new for 2025. It holds the four new deductions: tips, overtime, car loan interest and the senior deduction. They are subtracted after AGI and are available whether or not you itemize.
687|Schedule 3 collects nonrefundable credits other than the child credits (foreign tax, education, dependent care, saver's, energy) and other payments, such as tax paid with an extension and excess Social Security withheld.
688|Schedule A lists itemized deductions: medical costs above 7.5% of AGI, state and local taxes up to the cap, home mortgage and investment interest, gifts to charity, and disaster losses. You use it only when the total beats your standard deduction.
693|Schedule EIC is attached when the earned income credit is claimed with a qualifying child. It gives each child's name, Social Security number, age, relationship and months lived with the taxpayer.
697|Schedule R figures the credit for the elderly or the disabled. It starts with a base amount for the filing status and reduces it for nontaxable pensions and for AGI above a low limit.
699|Schedule 8812 figures the child tax credit, the $500 credit for other dependents, and the refundable Additional Child Tax Credit, which is based on earned income over $2,500.
720|The mortgage lender sends Form 1098 showing the mortgage interest and points you paid in the year, and often property tax paid from escrow. It supports the home mortgage interest deduction on Schedule A.
721|A college sends Form 1098-T showing tuition paid and scholarships received. It is the starting point for the education credits, but you must adjust for books and for which year amounts were really paid.
722|The loan servicer sends Form 1098-E when you paid $600 or more of student loan interest. It supports the deduction of up to $2,500.
723|A charity that receives a car, boat or plane worth more than $500 sends Form 1098-C. It shows what the charity sold the vehicle for, which is normally the limit of your deduction.
724|Anyone who bought health insurance through the Marketplace gets Form 1095-A. It shows the monthly premium, the benchmark plan premium and any advance credit, which are the numbers needed for Form 8962.
726|Form 2441 is used for the child and dependent care credit. It also reports tax-free dependent care benefits from an employer, so it is required even when you only received employer benefits and claim no credit.
727|Form 8863 figures both education credits. Part III is filled in for each student; Part I gives the refundable part of the American Opportunity credit and Part II the nonrefundable credits.
728|Form 8962 compares the premium tax credit you are entitled to, based on actual income, with the advance payments sent to your insurer. Too little advance gives extra credit; too much means repayment. It is required whenever advance payments were made.
729|Form 5695 figures the two home energy credits: the residential clean energy credit (solar and similar) and the energy efficient home improvement credit (insulation, windows, heat pumps).
730|Form 8936 figures the credits for new and used clean vehicles. It asks for the vehicle identification number and applies the income and price limits.
731|Form 8839 figures the adoption credit and also the amount of employer adoption assistance that can be excluded from income. It tracks expenses by year and by child.
732|After the IRS denies the earned income credit, child tax credit or American Opportunity credit, Form 8862 must be attached the next time you claim it, to show you now qualify. It is not needed if the denial was only a math error.
733|Form 8801 figures the credit for AMT paid in earlier years on timing items. The credit can be used in a year when regular tax is higher than the tentative minimum tax, and the rest carries forward.
734|Form 1116 figures the foreign tax credit. It limits the credit to the share of U.S. tax that relates to foreign income, by category of income, and tracks carryovers.
740|Form 4684 reports losses from casualties and thefts. Section A is for personal property, where the $100 and 10% of AGI reductions apply; Section B is for business and income-producing property.
741|Form 8283 is required when total gifts of property are over $500. Section A covers items up to $5,000. Section B is for items over $5,000 and needs an appraiser's signature and the charity's acknowledgment.
742|Form 4952 figures how much investment interest can be deducted this year, limited to net investment income, and how much carries forward. It is also where you elect to treat qualified dividends or capital gains as investment income.
750|Form 2106 is now used only by the few employees who can still deduct work expenses: reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis government officials and employees with impairment-related work expenses.
751|Form 8995 is the simple form for the 20% qualified business income deduction. It is used when taxable income is at or below the threshold, so the wage and property limits do not apply. Higher incomes use Form 8995-A.
759|The child tax credit lowers tax by up to $2,200 for each qualifying child under 17 who has a Social Security number. It shrinks at high incomes. If it is larger than the tax, part can be refunded through the Additional Child Tax Credit.
760|When the child tax credit is more than your tax, the unused part can be refunded, up to $1,700 per child. The refund is limited to 15% of earned income above $2,500, so a family with no earned income gets none.
761|A $500 credit for each dependent who cannot be counted for the child tax credit, for example a 17-year-old, a college student, or an elderly parent. It only reduces tax; nothing is refunded.
762|The earned income credit is a refundable credit meant to reward work at low pay. It rises with earnings up to a point, then phases out. It is larger with children and is denied when investment income is over the yearly limit.
763|The American Opportunity credit covers 100% of the first $2,000 and 25% of the next $2,000 of college costs, so up to $2,500 per student, for the first 4 years. Up to $1,000 of it is refundable.
764|The Lifetime Learning credit is 20% of up to $10,000 of tuition for everyone on the return together. It has no limit on years and covers graduate study and courses to improve job skills. It is not refundable.
765|A credit for part of what you pay for care of a child under 13, or a disabled spouse or dependent, so that you (and your spouse) can work or look for work. Both spouses need earned income.
766|The premium tax credit helps pay for health insurance bought through the Marketplace. Most people get it in advance, paid to the insurer. On the return, Form 8962 compares the advance with the credit based on actual income.
767|The adoption credit covers reasonable costs of adopting a child, such as fees, court costs and travel, up to $17,280 per child for 2025. Up to $5,000 can be refunded; the rest carries forward up to 5 years.
768|When another country taxes your income and the U.S. taxes it too, the foreign tax credit lowers U.S. tax by the foreign tax paid, up to the U.S. tax on that foreign income.
770|A 30% credit for the cost of solar electric, solar water heating, wind, geothermal and battery storage on a home. It is not refundable, but unused credit carries to later years. It ends for costs after 2025.
771|A 30% credit for energy-saving improvements to an existing home, with yearly caps: $1,200 in general and $2,000 for heat pumps. It does not carry forward. It ends after 2025.
773|If you paid AMT because of a timing item, you paid tax early. This credit gives it back in a later year by reducing regular tax down to the level of that year's tentative minimum tax.
774|Refundable credits are paid even when you owe no tax. For 2025 the list is: the earned income credit, the Additional Child Tax Credit, 40% of the American Opportunity credit, the premium tax credit, up to $5,000 of the adoption credit, and excess Social Security withheld.
775|Nonrefundable credits can take your tax down to zero, and anything left over is lost unless that credit has a carryover. Common ones: the credit for other dependents, Lifetime Learning, dependent care, saver's, foreign tax and energy credits.
776|The difference shows when the credit is bigger than the tax. Example: tax of $800 and a $1,000 credit. If nonrefundable, tax becomes zero and $200 is lost. If refundable, the IRS pays you the $200.
777|A deduction reduces the income that is taxed, so its value depends on your tax rate. A credit reduces the tax itself. Example in the 22% bracket: a $1,000 deduction saves $220; a $1,000 credit saves $1,000.
778|The standard deduction is a set amount you may subtract without proving any expenses. It depends on filing status and is higher for people who are 65 or older or blind. You choose it or itemized deductions, whichever is larger.
779|Itemized deductions are specific personal expenses the law allows, listed on Schedule A. You itemize only when their total is more than your standard deduction. A married person filing separately must itemize if the spouse itemizes.
780|An adjustment is subtracted from total income to arrive at AGI. Everyone gets it, even with the standard deduction. Because it lowers AGI, it can also help with limits based on AGI, such as the medical expense floor.
781|The SALT deduction is for state and local taxes: income tax or sales tax, plus property taxes. The total is capped at $40,000 for 2025 ($20,000 if married filing separately), and the cap shrinks for MAGI above $500,000.
782|Interest on a loan used to buy, build or improve your main home or a second home is an itemized deduction. It is allowed on up to $750,000 of such debt, and the loan must be secured by the home.
783|Gifts of money or property to qualified charities are an itemized deduction. The amount you can deduct in one year is limited to a percentage of AGI (60% for cash to public charities), and the extra carries forward 5 years.
784|Unreimbursed costs of medical and dental care for you, your spouse and dependents are an itemized deduction, but only the part above 7.5% of AGI. Health insurance paid with after-tax money counts.
785|A loss from a sudden event such as a fire, flood or storm. For personal property it is deductible only in a federally declared disaster area, after subtracting $100 per event and 10% of AGI.
786|Owners of pass-through businesses can deduct up to 20% of the business's net income. It is taken after AGI and is available with either the standard deduction or itemized deductions. At higher incomes it is limited by wages paid and by the type of business.
787|Interest paid on a loan used only for higher education can be deducted up to $2,500 a year as an adjustment to income, so no itemizing is needed. It phases out at higher incomes.
788|K-12 teachers, aides, counselors and principals who work at least 900 hours can deduct up to $300 of unreimbursed classroom costs as an adjustment to income.
789|Money you put into your own HSA outside of payroll is deducted as an adjustment to income. You must be covered by a high-deductible health plan and not be on Medicare.
790|A self-employed person has no employer to provide tax-free health insurance. This adjustment gives a similar result: premiums are deducted before AGI, limited to the profit of the business.
791|An employee pays half of Social Security and Medicare tax and the employer pays half and deducts it. A self-employed person pays both halves, so the law lets them deduct one half as an adjustment to income.
792|A new temporary deduction of $6,000 for each taxpayer who is 65 or older. It is in addition to the standard deduction and its age add-on, and it phases out as MAGI rises above $75,000, or $150,000 on a joint return.
793|A new temporary deduction that takes up to $25,000 of qualified tips out of taxable income for workers in jobs that customarily receive tips. The tips must still be reported, and payroll taxes still apply.
794|A new temporary deduction for the extra pay earned for overtime, meaning the half in time-and-a-half, up to $12,500 or $25,000 joint. Only overtime required by the Fair Labor Standards Act counts.
795|A new temporary deduction for interest on a loan taken after 2024 to buy a new personal vehicle that had its final assembly in the U.S. The limit is $10,000 a year, and it is available without itemizing.
796|Interest on money borrowed to buy taxable investments is an itemized deduction, but only up to the net investment income you report. Unused interest carries forward.
827|Qualified business income is the net profit from a U.S. business run as a sole proprietorship, partnership or S corporation. It does not include investment income or wages. The deduction is 20% of it, subject to limits.
828|SALT means state and local taxes. On Schedule A it covers income tax or sales tax plus real estate and personal property taxes, subject to the cap ($40,000 for 2025).
829|The Fair Labor Standards Act is the federal law that requires time-and-a-half for hours over 40 in a week. Only overtime required by this law qualifies for the new overtime deduction; overtime owed only under a state law or a union contract does not.
830|The One Big Beautiful Bill Act is the tax law signed on July 4, 2025. It made the 2017 tax cuts permanent and added new items such as the tips, overtime, car loan interest and senior deductions. Its 2025 changes are tested in this exam window.
831|The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 nearly doubled the standard deduction, removed personal exemptions, capped the SALT deduction and created the 20% business income deduction. Most of its individual rules were set to end after 2025 before the new law extended them.
832|The Affordable Care Act created the health insurance Marketplace and the premium tax credit, and also the 3.8% net investment income tax and the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax.
833|The advance premium tax credit is the premium tax credit paid during the year straight to the insurance company to lower monthly premiums. Anyone who received it must file a return with Form 8962 to reconcile it.
858|A phase-out reduces a tax benefit step by step as income rises through a range. Below the range you get the full benefit; above it, none. Example: the IRA deduction shrinks evenly across its $10,000 range.
859|When a loss, deduction or credit cannot be used this year, the law may let you use it in another year. Forward means a later year; back means an earlier year, by amending or claiming a refund. Each item has its own time limit.
860|Acquisition debt is a loan used to buy, build or substantially improve your main or second home and secured by that home. Only interest on this kind of debt qualifies for the home mortgage interest deduction.
861|Points are a fee paid to the lender at closing, each point equal to 1% of the loan, in exchange for a lower rate. They are treated as interest paid in advance.
862|For education credits, qualified expenses are tuition and fees the school requires for enrollment. The American Opportunity credit also counts required books and supplies. Living costs such as room, board and transportation never count.
