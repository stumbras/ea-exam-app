323|A C corporation's unused items follow different rules from an individual's. Capital losses go back 3 years and forward 5, and only against capital gains. Charitable gifts over the limit carry forward 5 years. Net operating losses carry forward without limit but can offset only 80% of a later year's income.
335|S status ends in two ways. The owners can choose to end it, if holders of more than half the shares agree. Or the corporation stops qualifying, for example by getting a 101st shareholder. After it ends, the corporation normally must wait 5 years to elect again.
336|When a corporation closes and hands out its assets, there are two levels of tax. The corporation is taxed as if it sold every asset at market value. Each shareholder then has gain or loss: the value received minus their stock basis. A parent closing a subsidiary it owns 80% or more pays no tax.
337|When a corporation buys back its own stock, the question is whether the shareholder really reduced their ownership. If yes (all shares redeemed, or a large enough drop), it is a sale with capital gain. If the shareholder's percentage barely changes, the payment is treated as a dividend.
338|Earnings and profits is a tax-law measure of how much a corporation could pay out without returning capital. It starts from taxable income and adjusts toward economic reality: add tax-exempt income, subtract federal income tax paid, and use slower depreciation.
339|A payment does not need to be called a dividend to be one. If a corporation gives a shareholder a benefit with no business reason, such as paying personal bills, paying too much salary, or lending money at no interest, the IRS treats the benefit as a dividend: taxable to the shareholder and not deductible by the corporation.
340|A limited partner is an investor, not a worker, so the share of partnership profit is not subject to self-employment tax. If the limited partner is paid a guaranteed payment for actual services, that payment is subject to self-employment tax.
341|A partnership must give each partner a Schedule K-1 by the due date of Form 1065: March 15 for a calendar year, or September 15 with an extension. Partners need it to file their own returns.
349|When a partner leaves and the partnership pays them out, gain arises only if the money received is more than the partner's outside basis. Being relieved of a share of partnership debt counts as money. Receiving property alone does not trigger gain; the basis moves to the property.
350|A loss on leaving a partnership is allowed only in a narrow case: the partner receives only cash, unrealized receivables and inventory, and their total is less than the partner's outside basis. If any other property is received, there is no loss; the leftover basis attaches to that property.
351|In a final payout the partner's whole outside basis must go somewhere. First subtract cash. Then receivables and inventory take the partnership's own basis. Whatever basis remains is assigned to the other property. Example: outside basis $50,000, cash $10,000, inventory with basis $15,000, and land: the land takes $25,000.
352|When a partner sells their interest, the price includes more than cash. The partner's share of partnership debt that the buyer takes over is added. Example: $40,000 cash and $10,000 of debt relief gives an amount realized of $50,000.
353|A partnership interest is a capital asset, so the sale normally gives capital gain or loss. But the part of the price paid for the partner's share of unrealized receivables and inventory (hot assets) is ordinary income, so a partner cannot convert ordinary income to capital gain by selling.
354|Partnership debt increases the partners' basis, so it must be divided among them. Recourse debt goes to the partner who would have to pay it if the partnership could not. Nonrecourse debt, where no partner is personally liable, is generally shared by profit ratio.
355|When a partner sells their entire interest, the partnership's year ends for that partner on the sale date. The partner reports their share of income up to that day. The partnership itself continues with its normal year for everyone else.
356|A partnership ends for tax only when no partner continues any part of its business in partnership form. Since 2018 a sale of 50% or more of the interests no longer ends it.
357|Partners can move their business into a corporation tax-free under Section 351, because together they own at least 80% of the new corporation right after the transfer. Their basis and holding period carry over into the stock.
358|An LLC that wants S corporation treatment files only Form 2553. By filing it, the LLC is treated as having also chosen to be taxed as a corporation, so a separate Form 8832 is not needed.
359|If the S election was filed late, relief is available when the corporation meant to be an S corporation from that date, has reasonable cause, and asks within 3 years and 75 days of the intended start date. Form 2553 is filed with a statement explaining the delay.
360|To give up S status voluntarily, shareholders owning more than half the shares must consent. If the revocation is filed by the 15th day of the 3rd month, it applies for the whole year. Filed later, it takes effect the next year, unless a specific future date is named.
361|If the corporation breaks an S rule, such as gaining a nonresident alien shareholder, the election ends on that very day. The year is split in two short years: an S corporation year up to the day before, and a C corporation year from the event.
362|After S status ends, the corporation generally cannot elect S again for 5 tax years unless the IRS agrees. If the termination was accidental and is fixed quickly, the IRS can treat the election as never having ended.
363|LIFO inventory holds old, low costs, so it hides profit. A C corporation that becomes an S corporation must add its LIFO reserve to income in its last C corporation year. The extra tax is paid in 4 equal yearly installments.
364|When losses have reduced a shareholder's loan basis, later profit rebuilds the loan basis first and stock basis second. If the corporation repays the loan while its basis is still reduced, part of the repayment is gain to the shareholder.
365|Losses that exceed a shareholder's basis are not lost; they wait until basis is restored. They stay with that shareholder and cannot be passed on by sale. If S status ends, they can still be used during a transition period of about one year if the shareholder adds basis.
366|Closing a C corporation is taxed twice. First the corporation pays tax as if it sold its assets at market value. Then each shareholder pays tax on what they receive minus their stock basis.
367|A partnership or S corporation return usually shows no tax, so the penalty for filing late is based on the number of owners: $255 for each partner or shareholder for each month late, up to 12 months, for 2025 returns. Example: 4 partners, 3 months late: $3,060.
368|A C corporation pays its own tax, so its late filing penalty works like an individual's: 5% of the unpaid tax per month up to 25%. Paying too little estimated tax is figured separately on Form 2220.
393|An LLC with one owner is ignored for income tax unless it elects otherwise. An individual owner reports the business on Schedule C, E or F, as if the LLC did not exist. It can choose to be taxed as a corporation with Form 8832.
394|An LLC with two or more owners is taxed as a partnership by default and files Form 1065. It can elect to be taxed as a corporation.
395|Form 8832 is how an eligible entity such as an LLC chooses its tax classification: corporation, partnership or disregarded entity. Once changed, it generally cannot be changed again for 60 months.
396|Form 2553 elects S corporation status. To apply for the current year it must be filed within 2 months and 15 days after the year starts (March 15 for a calendar year), or any time during the year before. Every shareholder must sign.
397|Only a small, simple U.S. corporation can be an S corporation: no more than 100 shareholders, all of them eligible (U.S. individuals, estates and certain trusts), and only one class of stock.
398|Certain owners make a corporation ineligible for S status: nonresident aliens, partnerships and other corporations. The idea is that the income must be taxed once to a U.S. individual. If one of them gets even one share, the election ends.
399|For the 100-shareholder limit, a married couple counts as one shareholder, and a whole family (up to six generations from a common ancestor) can be treated as one. So an S corporation can have well over 100 actual people as owners.
400|All shares must give the same rights to distributions and to assets on liquidation. Shares may differ in voting power, so voting and nonvoting common stock is allowed. Preferred stock is not.
401|Partnerships and S corporations file a month before individuals, so owners get their Schedule K-1 in time. For a calendar year the due date is March 15, and Form 7004 extends it 6 months to September 15.
402|A calendar-year C corporation files Form 1120 by April 15, the 15th day of the 4th month after year end. Form 7004 gives 6 more months, to October 15.
403|A C corporation pays one flat rate of 21% on all taxable income, with no brackets. The owners are taxed again when profit is paid out as dividends.
404|A personal holding company is a closely held corporation that earns mostly investment income. To stop people from using a corporation as a low-tax pocket for investments, a 20% tax is added on the income it does not pay out.
405|Starting a corporation should not be a taxable event. If the people transferring property own at least 80% of the stock right afterward, no gain or loss is recognized. If they also receive cash or other property (boot), gain is taxed up to that amount.
406|Putting property into a partnership in exchange for an interest is tax-free, with no 80% control requirement. The partnership takes over the partner's basis in the property, and the partner's interest gets that same basis.
407|Outside basis is the partner's running investment. It goes up for money and property contributed, for the partner's share of income (even tax-exempt income), and for any increase in their share of partnership debt.
408|Outside basis goes down for distributions, for the partner's share of losses and nondeductible expenses, and for any decrease in their share of partnership debt. It can never go below zero.
409|A partner's loss must pass four tests in order. First, it cannot exceed outside basis. Second, the amount at risk. Third, the passive activity rules. Fourth, the excess business loss limit. A loss stopped at any step is carried forward under that step's rules.
410|A distribution of money is tax-free until it exceeds the partner's outside basis. The extra is capital gain. Example: basis $10,000, cash distribution $14,000: $4,000 capital gain and basis becomes zero.
411|When a partnership distributes property to a continuing partner, the partner normally takes the partnership's basis in it. But it cannot be more than the partner's outside basis after subtracting any cash received in the same distribution.
412|Hot assets are the partnership items that would produce ordinary income: unrealized receivables (including depreciation recapture) and inventory. When a partner sells an interest, the gain tied to them is ordinary income, not capital gain.
413|A partnership cannot freely pick its tax year. It must use the year of partners owning more than 50%. If there is none, the year of all the principal partners (5% or more). If still none, the year that defers the least income.
414|Since 2018 the IRS audits a partnership at the partnership level and collects any tax from the partnership itself. The partnership names a partnership representative with full authority to act. A partnership with 100 or fewer eligible partners can elect out each year on its return.
415|An S corporation shareholder adjusts stock basis each year in a set order: first add income, then subtract distributions, then nondeductible expenses, then losses. Because distributions come before losses, a distribution is more likely to be tax-free.
416|A shareholder can deduct losses only up to stock basis plus loans the shareholder personally made to the corporation. Guaranteeing a bank loan to the corporation creates no basis, unlike a partner, who gets basis from partnership debt.
417|If the S corporation never had C corporation earnings, a distribution is simply a return of the shareholder's investment: tax-free up to stock basis, and capital gain above it.
418|An S corporation that was once a C corporation may still hold old earnings. Distributions come first from the account of income already taxed to shareholders (AAA), which is tax-free. Next they come from the old earnings, taxed as dividends. After that they reduce basis.
419|S corporation profit passed through to owners is not subject to payroll tax, so owners are tempted to take no salary. The IRS requires an owner who works in the business to be paid a reasonable wage, with payroll taxes, before taking distributions.
420|Health insurance the S corporation pays for an owner of more than 2% is added to that owner's wages for income tax, but not for Social Security and Medicare. The owner can then deduct it as self-employed health insurance.
421|This applies only to an S corporation that has old C corporation earnings. If more than 25% of its gross receipts are passive investment income, it pays a corporate-level tax on the excess. If that happens 3 years in a row, the S election ends.
422|A C corporation can deduct charitable gifts only up to 10% of its taxable income, figured before the gift deduction and the dividends-received deduction. Any excess carries forward 5 years.
423|A corporation gets no $3,000 allowance. Its capital losses can be used only against capital gains. Unused losses go back 3 years and then forward 5 years, treated as short-term.
424|A net operating loss from recent years cannot be carried back, except for farm losses (2 years). It carries forward with no time limit, but in any later year it can offset only 80% of taxable income.
425|A corporation must pay estimated tax if it expects to owe $500 or more. Payments are due on the 15th day of the 4th, 6th, 9th and 12th months of its year. A large corporation cannot rely on last year's tax as a safe harbor, except for the first payment.
426|Income on the financial books differs from taxable income. Schedule M-1 explains the difference, for example federal tax expense, meals and tax-exempt interest. Corporations with total assets of $10 million or more must use the more detailed Schedule M-3.
427|Schedule M-2 shows how retained earnings changed during the year: start with the opening balance, add net income per books, subtract dividends, and arrive at the closing balance.
428|A distribution to a shareholder is taxed in three layers. It is a dividend up to the corporation's earnings and profits, current year first, then accumulated. Next it is a tax-free return of capital that lowers stock basis. Anything beyond basis is capital gain.
429|If a corporation lends money to a shareholder at little or no interest, the tax law creates the missing interest. The unpaid interest is treated as a dividend to the shareholder. If the borrower is an employee, it is treated as pay.
430|Stock losses are normally capital losses. Section 1244 lets the first owner of stock in a small corporation deduct a loss as ordinary, up to $50,000 a year or $100,000 on a joint return, which is far more useful.
431|Owners of sole proprietorships, partnerships and S corporations can deduct 20% of their qualified business income. It was created to balance the 21% corporate rate, and the new law made it permanent. C corporations do not get it.
432|Below the taxable income threshold ($197,300, or $394,600 joint, for 2025) the 20% deduction is simple. Above it, limits phase in across the next $50,000 ($100,000 joint): the wage and property limit, and the loss of the deduction for service businesses.
433|For higher-income owners the deduction cannot exceed the greater of two amounts: 50% of the W-2 wages the business paid, or 25% of wages plus 2.5% of the original cost of its depreciable property. A business with no employees and no property gets nothing above the range.
434|Form 8995 is the short version for taxpayers at or below the income threshold. Above it, Form 8995-A is required because the wage and property limits and the service business rules must be applied.
435|A specified service business sells the skill of its people: health, law, accounting, consulting, performing arts, athletics, financial services. Once taxable income is above the phase-in range, these businesses get no 20% deduction. Engineers and architects are not on the list.
436|A business can deduct losses; a hobby cannot. If the activity made a profit in 3 of the last 5 years (2 of 7 for horse breeding), it is presumed to be a business. A hobby must report its income and cannot deduct its expenses.
437|The cash method is simple but lets income be shifted between years. So C corporations, and partnerships with a C corporation partner, must use accrual unless their average gross receipts are $31 million or less.
438|A business with average yearly gross receipts of $31 million or less over the prior 3 years counts as small. It may use the cash method, skip inventory capitalization rules, and is not subject to the business interest limit.
439|A business cannot simply switch how it reports. Changing an accounting method, for example cash to accrual, requires Form 3115. Changing the tax year requires Form 1128.
440|A larger business can deduct interest only up to its interest income plus 30% of adjusted taxable income. For 2025 that income is figured before depreciation and amortization, which raises the limit. Interest not allowed carries forward. Small businesses under the gross receipts test are exempt.
441|An individual cannot use unlimited business losses against wages and investment income. For 2025 the net business loss allowed is $313,000, or $626,000 on a joint return. The excess becomes a net operating loss carried to later years.
442|Gain on stock of a qualifying small C corporation, bought at original issue, can be excluded from income. The classic rule requires holding more than 5 years. The new law added partial exclusions at 3 and 4 years and a larger cap for stock issued after its enactment.
869|Form 1065 is the partnership's information return. The partnership pays no income tax; the form reports its income and deductions and gives each partner a Schedule K-1 with their share.
870|Form 1120 is the income tax return of a C corporation. The corporation figures and pays its own tax at 21%.
871|Form 1120-S is the return of an S corporation. Like a partnership it generally pays no tax; income and deductions pass to shareholders on Schedule K-1 in proportion to their shares.
872|Form 7004 gives an automatic extension to file business returns, usually 6 months. It does not extend the time to pay any tax due.
873|Form SS-4 is the application for an employer identification number. A business needs one to hire employees, open a business bank account or file business returns.
874|Form 3115 asks the IRS for permission to change an accounting method, such as cash to accrual or a change in depreciation. Many changes are automatic if the form is filed correctly.
875|Form 1128 is used to adopt, change or keep a tax year, for example to move from a calendar year to a fiscal year.
876|Form 8990 figures how much business interest expense can be deducted under the 30% limit and how much carries forward. Small businesses under the gross receipts test do not file it.
877|Form 461 figures the excess business loss of an individual: the amount of net business loss above the yearly limit, which is carried forward as a net operating loss.
878|Form 1125-A figures cost of goods sold: opening inventory plus purchases and production costs, minus closing inventory. It is attached to Forms 1065, 1120 and 1120-S.
879|A corporation uses Form 2220 to figure the penalty for paying too little estimated tax during the year.
880|Form 1120-W is a worksheet a corporation uses to estimate its tax and the four required installments. It is not filed with the IRS.
881|An S corporation shareholder uses Form 7203 to track stock basis and loan basis. It must be attached when the shareholder claims a loss, receives a distribution, sells stock or is repaid a loan.
903|A limited liability company is formed under state law to protect its owners from the business's debts. It has no tax classification of its own: one owner means it is ignored, two or more means partnership, unless it elects to be taxed as a corporation.
904|A personal service corporation is a C corporation whose main activity is services by its employee-owners in fields like health, law, engineering or accounting. It generally must use a calendar year and has a lower accumulated earnings credit.
905|The accumulated adjustments account tracks S corporation income that has already been taxed to shareholders but not yet paid out. It matters when the corporation also has old C corporation earnings: distributions from this account come out first and are tax-free.
906|The dividends-received deduction prevents the same profit from being taxed at three levels. A C corporation that receives a dividend from another U.S. corporation deducts 50% of it (owning under 20%), 65% (20% up to 80%) or 100% (80% or more).
907|The accumulated earnings tax is a 20% penalty on a C corporation that keeps profits beyond what the business reasonably needs, in order to spare shareholders the tax on dividends. The first $250,000 ($150,000 for service corporations) is protected.
908|A personal holding company is a corporation in which 5 or fewer people own more than half the stock and at least 60% of its income is passive, such as dividends, interest and rents. It pays an extra 20% tax on income it does not distribute.
909|When a C corporation becomes an S corporation, appreciation that built up during the C years is tracked. If those assets are sold within 5 years, the S corporation itself pays tax at 21% on that built-in gain.
910|A specified service trade or business is one based on personal skill, such as medicine, law, accounting or consulting. Its owners lose the 20% business income deduction once taxable income exceeds the phase-in range.
911|UBIA is the original cost of depreciable property the business still uses, without subtracting depreciation. 2.5% of it is part of the alternative limit on the 20% deduction, which helps businesses with much property and few employees, like real estate.
912|Adjusted taxable income is the base for the business interest limit: 30% of it, plus interest income, is the most interest a business can deduct. For 2025 it is figured before interest, depreciation and amortization.
913|A qualified subchapter S subsidiary is a corporation owned 100% by an S corporation that elects this status. For tax it does not exist separately: its income and assets are treated as the parent's.
914|Earnings and profits measure how much a corporation can distribute as dividends. A distribution is a taxable dividend only up to this amount; beyond it, the payment is a return of capital.
915|A pass-through entity does not pay income tax itself. Its income, deductions and credits pass to the owners, who report them on their own returns. Partnerships and S corporations are the main examples.
916|Some items must be shown separately on Schedule K-1 because their tax treatment depends on each owner's own situation: capital gains and losses, Section 179 deduction, charitable gifts, interest and dividends. Everything else is combined into ordinary business income.
917|Outside basis is each partner's basis in their partnership interest. Inside basis is the partnership's basis in the assets it owns. They start out equal but can differ after someone buys an interest, which is why a Section 754 election exists.
918|The distributive share is the part of partnership income, loss and credits assigned to a partner by the agreement. The partner is taxed on it when the partnership earns it, even if no money is paid out.
919|Under constructive receipt, income belongs to the year it was made available to you without real restrictions. A cash-method business cannot delay income by leaving a check uncashed in December.
920|A fiscal year is a tax year of 12 months ending on the last day of any month other than December, for example July 1 to June 30. C corporations can choose one freely; pass-through entities are restricted.
921|Under the cash method, income counts when money is actually or constructively received and expenses count when paid. It is simple and is allowed for most small businesses.
922|Under the accrual method, income counts when you have the right to it and expenses when you owe them, regardless of when cash moves. Example: an invoice sent in December and paid in January is December income.
