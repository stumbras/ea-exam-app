389|Appeals is there to settle disputes, not to audit again. It normally will not bring up new issues or reopen points the taxpayer already agreed with the examiner. It looks at the hazards of litigation: how likely each side is to win in court, and settles accordingly.
390|A case is non-docketed when the taxpayer goes to Appeals after the 30-day letter, before any court filing. It is docketed when the taxpayer has already filed a petition in Tax Court; the court case is then usually sent to Appeals to try to settle before trial.
590|Form 2848 gives a named person the power to act for the taxpayer before the IRS: get confidential information, argue the case, and sign agreements and consents. It does not by default allow signing the return or cashing a refund check.
591|Only individual people who are eligible to practice can be named on Form 2848, never a firm. The form must be specific: the type of tax, the form number, and the years or periods covered. A form that says all years is not accepted.
592|An authorization can cover any past period, but future periods only up to 3 years after December 31 of the year the IRS receives the form. Example: received in 2026: it can cover periods through 2029.
593|Form 8821 lets a person or a company see and receive the taxpayer's confidential tax information. That is all: the person cannot speak for the taxpayer, argue a position or sign anything. It is often used by lenders and by preparers who only need transcripts.
594|The Centralized Authorization File is the IRS computer record of who is authorized for which taxpayer. Each representative is given a CAF number to put on every Form 2848 or 8821 so the IRS can find the authorization.
595|To cancel a power of attorney, the taxpayer writes REVOKE across the top of a copy of the form, signs and dates it, and sends it to the IRS. Filing a new Form 2848 for the same matter and period also cancels the old one automatically, unless the box to keep the earlier representative is checked.
596|A return must normally be signed by the taxpayer. A representative can sign it only in narrow cases: the taxpayer has a disease or injury, has been outside the United States for at least 60 days before the due date, or has other good cause approved by the IRS. The power of attorney must be attached.
597|A power of attorney is personal to each taxpayer. Even when spouses file a joint return, each must sign their own Form 2848 to appoint a representative.
598|A fiduciary, such as an executor, trustee or guardian, is not a representative; the fiduciary takes the place of the taxpayer. Form 56 tells the IRS about this relationship, and no Form 2848 is needed for the fiduciary to act.
599|By checking a box on the return, the taxpayer lets one named person, often the preparer, talk to the IRS about how that return is processed: missing information, refund status, math errors. It ends one year after the return's original due date and does not allow representation in an audit.
600|An enrolled agent can represent any taxpayer, on any tax matter, before every part of the IRS, including examination, collection and Appeals. The IRS is not a court, though: to appear in Tax Court a non-attorney must pass that court's own exam.
601|The IRS must normally work through the representative. If the representative is causing unreasonable delay or blocking the examination or collection, the employee can get a supervisor's approval to contact the taxpayer directly.
602|A taxpayer does not have to attend an IRS interview personally if a representative with a power of attorney goes, unless the IRS issues a summons. If the taxpayer says during an interview that they want to consult a representative, the interview must stop.
603|There are three kinds of audit. A correspondence audit is done by mail on a few simple items. An office audit takes place at an IRS office. A field audit is the most thorough, with a revenue agent visiting the taxpayer's home or business.
604|The IRS computer compares what is on a return with the Forms W-2 and 1099 it received. If income seems to be missing, it sends notice CP2000 proposing more tax. This is a matching notice, not an examination, and the taxpayer may agree or explain.
605|After an examination the IRS sends a report of its proposed changes with a letter giving 30 days to respond. Within those 30 days the taxpayer can agree, or ask for a conference with the independent Appeals office.
606|If the taxpayer does not agree and the case is not settled, the IRS issues a formal notice of deficiency. From its date the taxpayer has 90 days (150 if abroad) to file a petition in Tax Court without paying first. The IRS cannot assess the tax during that time. The deadline cannot be extended.
607|To go to Appeals, a formal written protest is required when the total disputed amount for any one tax period is more than $25,000. For $25,000 or less, a brief written statement or the small case request form is enough.
608|If Appeals and the taxpayer cannot agree in a case that has not been filed in court, Appeals issues the notice of deficiency. The taxpayer then has 90 days to petition the Tax Court.
609|For disputes of $50,000 or less per year, including penalties, the taxpayer can choose the Tax Court's simplified small case procedure. It is quicker and less formal, but the decision is final: neither side can appeal, and it does not set precedent.
610|Other courts hear tax cases only as refund suits. The taxpayer must first pay the full tax, then file a refund claim, and can sue after the IRS denies it or 6 months pass. A U.S. District Court offers a jury; the Court of Federal Claims does not.
611|The IRS generally has 3 years to assess more tax, counted from the due date or the date the return was filed, whichever is later. It has 6 years if more than 25% of gross income was left off. There is no limit when no return was filed or the return was fraudulent.
612|Filing early does not start the clock early. A return filed before the due date is treated as filed on the due date, both for the IRS's time to assess and for the taxpayer's time to claim a refund.
613|When an audit cannot finish in time, the IRS asks the taxpayer to agree to more time. Form 872 extends the period to a specific date. Form 872-A leaves it open until either side ends it with Form 872-T. The taxpayer can refuse or limit the extension to certain issues.
614|A refund claim must be filed within 3 years from when the return was filed or 2 years from when the tax was paid, whichever is later. The amount that can be refunded is also limited: under the 3-year rule, to tax paid in the 3 years plus any extension before the claim.
615|The form depends on what is being claimed. Individuals amend with Form 1040-X and corporations with Form 1120-X. Form 843 is for asking the IRS to remove or refund penalties, interest and certain other taxes, not income tax.
616|Audit reconsideration lets a taxpayer ask the IRS to look again at an audit result that is already assessed, typically because they never responded or now have documents they did not have before. The tax must still be unpaid, and it is not a formal appeal right.
617|Normally the taxpayer must prove the return is right. In court the burden moves to the IRS on a factual issue if the taxpayer presents credible evidence, kept the required records, backed up the items, and cooperated with reasonable IRS requests.
618|First-time abatement removes a late filing, late payment or late deposit penalty without needing a reason, as a reward for a clean record. The taxpayer must have had no significant penalties in the prior 3 years, have filed all required returns, and have paid or arranged to pay the tax.
619|A penalty can be removed if the taxpayer acted with ordinary care but still could not comply, for example because of a death or serious illness in the family, a fire or disaster, or being unable to get records. Lack of money or not knowing the law is normally not enough.
620|A penalty can be challenged at three points. Before it is assessed, by answering the notice or going to Appeals. After it is assessed, by asking for abatement by letter or Form 843. After it is paid, by filing a claim for refund, and suing if the claim is denied.
621|Interest is a charge for the use of money, so it is almost never removed. The exception is interest caused by an unreasonable error or delay by an IRS employee in a routine procedural or managerial act, such as losing a file. Reasonable cause does not remove interest.
622|The IRS pays interest on refunds, but has a grace period: no interest if the refund is issued within 45 days after the return's due date, or after the filing date if the return was filed late.
623|The Taxpayer Advocate Service is an independent office inside the IRS. It helps when a taxpayer faces real hardship or cannot get a problem fixed through normal channels. Help is requested on Form 911, and the Advocate can issue an order requiring the IRS to act or stop.
624|The Taxpayer Bill of Rights gathers 10 rights found throughout the tax code, such as the right to be informed, to quality service, to pay no more than the correct tax, to challenge the IRS and be heard, to appeal, to privacy and confidentiality, and to keep representation.
625|Before the IRS contacts other people, such as banks, employers or neighbors, about a taxpayer's liability, it must notify the taxpayer in advance. The notice must be sent at least 45 days before the contact and covers a period of up to one year.
626|A summons is an IRS order to appear, give testimony or produce records. When it is served on a third party, like a bank, the taxpayer gets notice and has 20 days to ask a court to cancel it.
627|These programs settle disagreements faster. An Appeals officer acts as a neutral mediator while the case stays with the examiner or collection officer. The goal is resolution within about 60 days for small business cases. Taking part is voluntary and no appeal rights are lost.
954|Form 911 asks the Taxpayer Advocate Service for help when IRS action or inaction is causing financial difficulty or a problem has not been solved through normal channels.
955|Form 843 is used to claim a refund or ask for abatement of penalties, interest or certain taxes other than income tax. It is the usual form for a penalty abatement request.
956|By signing Form 872 the taxpayer agrees to give the IRS more time to assess tax, up to a specific date written on the form.
957|Form 872-A extends the time to assess tax with no fixed end date. It stays open until 90 days after either the taxpayer or the IRS ends it with Form 872-T.
958|By signing Form 870 the taxpayer agrees with the examination changes and lets the IRS assess the tax right away, without a notice of deficiency. This stops more interest from building up. The taxpayer can still pay and file a refund claim later.
959|Form 4549 is the examiner's report. It lists each adjustment to income, deductions and credits, and the resulting tax, penalties and interest.
960|Form 12203 is the short way to ask for an Appeals conference when the disputed amount is $25,000 or less for each period. It lists the items the taxpayer disagrees with and why.
961|Form 4506-T requests a free transcript, a computer summary of a return or account. Most people who need proof of income or filing use this.
962|Form 4506 requests an actual photocopy of a filed return with its attachments. There is a fee and it takes longer than a transcript.
998|A power of attorney is the taxpayer's written authorization, on Form 2848, for an eligible person to represent them before the IRS and act in their place.
999|A tax information authorization, Form 8821, lets the named person or business receive and look at the taxpayer's tax information. It gives no right to represent the taxpayer.
1000|The Taxpayer Advocate Service helps taxpayers who are suffering hardship or whose problems the IRS has not resolved. It is independent of the IRS offices it deals with and its help is free.
1001|The Taxpayer Bill of Rights is the list of 10 fundamental rights taxpayers have when dealing with the IRS. IRS employees are required to act in keeping with them.
1002|The statutory notice of deficiency is the IRS's formal claim that more tax is owed. It is the ticket to Tax Court: the taxpayer has 90 days to petition, and the IRS cannot assess or collect that tax during this time.
1003|The Automated Underreporter program is the IRS's computer matching of returns against Forms W-2 and 1099. A mismatch produces a CP2000 notice proposing additional tax.
1004|The Discriminant Function is a computer score given to each return showing how likely an audit is to find more tax. Returns with high scores are reviewed by people and may be chosen for examination.
1005|First-time abatement is an administrative waiver of certain penalties for a taxpayer with a clean record for the prior 3 years. No reasonable cause has to be shown.
1006|The assessment statute expiration date is the last day the IRS can legally assess additional tax for a year, normally 3 years after the return was filed or due.
1007|The refund statute expiration date is the last day a taxpayer can claim a refund: generally 3 years from filing or 2 years from payment, whichever is later.
1008|The Internal Revenue Manual is the IRS's handbook of instructions for its own employees. It is useful for understanding IRS procedures, but it is not law and is not authority for a tax position.
1009|A summons is the IRS's legal tool to require a person to appear, testify or hand over books and records. If ignored, the IRS can ask a federal court to enforce it.
1010|A protest is the taxpayer's written statement to Appeals. It lists the adjustments being disputed, the facts, and the law the taxpayer relies on. It is required when the disputed amount is over $25,000 for a period.
1011|The practitioner privilege keeps tax advice between a taxpayer and a federally authorized tax practitioner confidential, in the same way as with an attorney, but only in noncriminal tax matters before the IRS or in federal court.
