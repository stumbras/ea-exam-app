0|The standard deduction is a flat amount everyone may subtract in place of itemizing. For 2025 a single person or a married person filing separately gets $15,750. The new law raised it during the year, so the older $15,000 figure is a wrong answer choice on this exam.
1|A married couple filing jointly gets double the single amount: $15,750 × 2 = $31,500. A qualifying surviving spouse uses the same amount as a joint return.
2|Head of Household sits between single and joint because the person supports a home for someone else. For 2025 it is $23,625, which is one and a half times the single amount.
3|People who are 65 or older, or blind, get an extra amount added to the regular standard deduction. Unmarried people get $2,000 for each condition; married people get $1,600 each. Example: a single person who is 67 and blind adds $4,000. A married couple both over 65 adds $3,200.
4|A dependent, such as a teenager with a job, gets a smaller standard deduction so parents cannot shelter investment income in a child's name. It is the larger of $1,350 or earned income plus $450, but never more than the normal amount. Example: wages of $3,000 give $3,450.
5|A married person filing separately must file with only $5 of gross income. The reason: if the other spouse itemizes, this spouse gets no standard deduction, so almost any income is taxable.
6|Self-employment tax pays for Social Security and Medicare, and it starts at $400 of net profit. So a person with $400 or more of net self-employment earnings must file, even if total income is far below the normal filing threshold.
7|Churches may be exempt from paying employer Social Security tax. Their employees then pay self-employment tax on those wages themselves, and must file once the wages reach $108.28.
8|Your filing status depends on whether you are married on the last day of the year. Marry on December 31 and you are married for the whole year. Divorce by December 31 and you are unmarried for the whole year. If a spouse dies, the survivor is still treated as married for that year.
9|After a spouse dies, the survivor files jointly for the year of death. For the next 2 years, a survivor who keeps a home for a dependent child and has not remarried can use Qualifying Surviving Spouse, which gives joint return rates and standard deduction. Example: spouse dies in 2025: joint for 2025, QSS for 2026 and 2027.
10|Head of Household is for an unmarried person who carries the main cost of a home for a child or relative. The test is money: you must pay more than half of the household's yearly costs.
11|Only the costs of running the home itself count: rent, mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, repairs, utilities and food eaten at home. Personal costs such as clothing, schooling, medical bills and vacations do not count. Mortgage principal does not count either.
12|To be your qualifying child a person must pass every test: be related to you in the right way, be young enough, live with you more than half the year, not pay more than half of their own support, and not file a joint return. Notice the support test looks at what the child paid, not what you paid.
13|The child must be under 19 at the end of the year, or under 24 if a full-time student, and younger than you. There is no age limit for a child who is permanently and totally disabled. Example: a 22-year-old full-time student passes; a 22-year-old who works and is not in school fails.
14|A student counts as full-time for the year if enrolled full time during some part of 5 calendar months. The months do not need to be in a row. Example: January to May is enough, even after graduating in May.
15|A qualifying relative is a dependent who is not a qualifying child, such as a parent or an adult child. Four tests: not anyone's qualifying child; either related to you or living with you all year; gross income under $5,200; and you pay more than half of their support. There is no age test.
16|A qualifying relative must have gross income under $5,200 for 2025. Only taxable income counts, so Social Security that is not taxable is ignored. Example: a mother with $4,000 of interest and $14,000 of nontaxable Social Security passes this test.
17|Normally the parent the child lives with claims the child. With Form 8332 that parent lets the other parent claim the child. Only the child tax credit and the dependency claim move. Head of Household, the earned income credit and the dependent care credit always stay with the parent the child lives with.
18|For divorced or separated parents, the custodial parent is the one the child slept at for more nights in the year. It is counted in nights, not decided by the divorce decree. If the nights are equal, it is the parent with the higher AGI.
19|When both parents could claim the same child and they do not file together, the child belongs to the parent the child lived with longer. If the time is exactly equal, the parent with the higher AGI wins.
20|A parent always has the first right to claim their own child. Someone else, like a grandparent, can claim the child only if no parent does and the other person's AGI is higher than the AGI of either parent.
21|A dependent must be a U.S. citizen, national or resident, or live in Canada or Mexico. A child living in another country, such as a nephew in Lithuania, cannot be claimed even if you pay all of the support.
22|A person who can be claimed as someone's dependent cannot claim dependents of their own. Example: a 19-year-old mother who is her parents' dependent cannot claim her baby; her parents may claim both.
23|A nonresident alien normally cannot file a joint return. A U.S. citizen or resident married to one can elect to treat the spouse as a resident. They can then file jointly, but the price is that both spouses' income from the whole world becomes taxable here.
24|An ITIN is a tax number for someone who must file or be listed on a return but cannot get a Social Security number. Form W-7 is the application, usually sent with the tax return and identity documents.
25|During an adoption the child may not have a Social Security number yet. Form W-7A gets a temporary number, the ATIN, so the adopting parents can claim the child. An ATIN does not work for the earned income credit.
26|Some benefits are claimed wrongly so often that paid preparers must ask extra questions and keep records. Form 8867 is the checklist. It covers the earned income credit, the child tax credit family, the American Opportunity credit, and Head of Household status. The penalty is charged per failure.
27|A child or relative who is away for a temporary reason is still treated as living with you. School, hospital stays, military service, vacation and juvenile detention all count as time at home. So a college student in a dorm still meets the residency test.
28|A baby born in December, or a child who died during the year, is treated as living with you the whole year, as long as your home was the child's home for the time the child was alive.
159|A single person under 65 must file when gross income reaches the standard deduction, $15,750. At 65 the extra $2,000 standard deduction raises the threshold to $17,750. The idea: below your standard deduction, no tax can be due.
160|For a joint return the threshold is the joint standard deduction, $31,500, plus $1,600 for each spouse who is 65 or older: $33,100 with one, $34,700 with both.
161|For Head of Household the threshold equals the standard deduction: $23,625, or $25,625 if 65 or older (adding $2,000).
162|A qualifying surviving spouse uses the joint amount, $31,500. At 65 only one extra amount of $1,600 is added, giving $33,100, because there is only one person.
163|Being blind raises the standard deduction but does not change the gross income level at which you must file. Only being 65 or older raises the filing threshold. This is a common trick question.
164|A dependent has lower filing thresholds because of the smaller standard deduction. A single dependent must file with more than $1,350 of unearned income (interest, dividends), or more than $15,750 of wages. With both kinds, compare total income with the larger of $1,350 or earned income plus $450.
165|Some people must file even with very little income, because they owe a tax that is not income tax or must reconcile something. Main cases: self-employment profit of $400 or more, the 10% penalty on a retirement withdrawal, HSA distributions, and advance premium tax credit payments received.
166|Not being required to file is different from not benefiting from filing. A person with small wages should still file to get back withheld tax or to claim refundable credits like the earned income credit.
167|The tax law treats you as reaching an age on the day before your birthday. So a person born January 1, 1961 is considered 65 on December 31, 2025 and gets the age-65 amounts for 2025.
168|Last year's return shows amounts that carry into this year: unused capital losses, charitable gifts over the limit, passive losses, depreciation taken so far, and overpayments applied to this year. It also shows whether a state refund is taxable.
169|Form 8822 tells the IRS a new home address, so notices reach you. Form 8822-B is the business version; it also reports a change in the responsible person for an EIN.
170|If someone used your Social Security number to file a false return, you send Form 14039 to report the identity theft. The IRS then investigates and usually issues an Identity Protection PIN for later years.
171|An IP PIN is a 6-digit number the IRS gives to protect a person from identity theft. If anyone on the return has one, it must be entered or an e-filed return is rejected. A new number is issued every year.
172|Credits have different ID rules. The earned income credit needs valid Social Security numbers for everyone. The child tax credit needs a Social Security number for the child and, from 2025, for at least one parent. The $500 credit for other dependents accepts an ITIN.
173|The child must be your own child, stepchild, foster child, brother or sister (including half and step), or a descendant of one of them. So a grandchild, niece or nephew can be your qualifying child. A cousin cannot.
174|Close relatives can be your qualifying relative without living with you: children, parents, grandparents, siblings, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews and in-laws. A cousin or an unrelated person, such as a girlfriend, must live in your home the entire year.
175|Support means what was spent on the person's living needs: food, housing (at its rental value), clothing, education, medical care and transportation. Scholarships received by a student who is your child are ignored. Compare what you paid with the total from all sources, including the person's own money.
176|A child kidnapped by someone outside the family is still treated as living with the parent for the tests, if the child lived with the parent more than half of the part of the year before the kidnapping.
177|A parent is the one exception to the rule that the qualifying person must live with you for Head of Household. If you pay more than half the cost of your parent's home, or nursing home, and can claim the parent as a dependent, you qualify.
178|For Head of Household the qualifying person normally must be your dependent. Exception: your unmarried qualifying child counts even if you cannot claim the child, for example because you gave the claim to the other parent with Form 8332.
179|A citizen married to a nonresident alien is treated as unmarried for Head of Household purposes if they do not elect to file jointly. With a qualifying child or relative, they can file as Head of Household. The nonresident spouse cannot be the qualifying person.
270|A person with a green card is a U.S. tax resident, taxed on income from the whole world, even if they spend little time here. Residency begins on the first day they are in the U.S. as a permanent resident.
271|Someone who arrives late in the year may not meet the 183-day test until next year. The first-year choice lets them be treated as a resident from their arrival this year, if they were here at least 31 days in a row. This can allow deductions and a joint return sooner.
272|Federal tax law follows state law on who is married. A marriage valid where it was performed counts, including same-sex and common-law marriages. Registered domestic partnerships and civil unions are not marriages for tax, so those partners file as single or Head of Household.
273|A divorce ends a marriage; an annulment says it never existed. So after an annulment you were never married for tax purposes and must amend the open years that were filed as married, usually the last 3 years.
274|A married person who files a joint return usually cannot be claimed as a dependent. Exception: the couple filed jointly only to get a refund of withheld tax, and neither would owe tax on separate returns.
275|A foster child counts as your child for the relationship test only if placed with you by an authorized agency or a court. A child you informally take in does not qualify under this rule.
276|When a person uses their own Social Security benefits to pay for living costs, that money counts as support they provided themselves. It can cause you to fail the more-than-half support test for a parent.
277|An alien who is leaving the United States may need a certificate showing their tax is paid, called a sailing permit. Form 1040-C is used to get it. It is not the final return; the person still files a regular return after year end.
278|A full-year resident of Puerto Rico does not pay U.S. income tax on income from Puerto Rico sources. Income from outside Puerto Rico, such as U.S. wages or U.S. dividends, is still reported on Form 1040.
279|Certain people do not count their days in the U.S. toward the 183-day test, such as students and teachers on certain visas. Form 8843 is how they tell the IRS they are excluding those days. It is filed even when there is no income.
324|Two credits use different child rules. For the earned income credit the child can be up to 18 (23 if a student) and must live with you in the U.S. over half the year. For the child tax credit the child must be under 17 at year end. A 17-year-old can give you the earned income credit but not the child tax credit.
680|Form 1040 is the main yearly income tax return for U.S. citizens and residents. Income, deductions, tax and credits are summarized on it, with details on attached schedules.
681|Form 1040-SR is the same return as Form 1040 with larger print and a standard deduction chart, for people 65 or older. Using it is optional and the tax is identical.
704|A business asks for Form W-9 to get your correct name and tax ID number so it can send you a Form 1099. The form goes to the payer, not the IRS. Refusing can lead to backup withholding of 24%.
725|When several people together support someone and no one pays over half, they can agree that one of them claims the dependent. That person must have paid more than 10%. Form 2120 is filed by the one who claims the dependent.
797|AGI is your total income minus certain adjustments such as IRA contributions, student loan interest and half of self-employment tax. Many limits are based on it: for example, medical expenses count only above 7.5% of AGI.
798|MAGI is AGI with some items added back, such as the foreign earned income exclusion or tax-exempt interest. There is no single definition: each rule (Roth IRA, premium tax credit, education credits) has its own list of add-backs.
799|Married Filing Jointly: both spouses report all income and deductions on one return. It usually gives the lowest tax, but each spouse is responsible for the whole tax bill.
800|Married Filing Separately: each spouse files their own return. Rates are less favorable and many benefits are lost, such as education credits, and usually the earned income credit. If one spouse itemizes, the other must too.
801|Head of Household: for an unmarried person who pays more than half the cost of a home for a qualifying person. It gives a bigger standard deduction and wider tax brackets than Single.
802|Qualifying Surviving Spouse: for the 2 years after the year a spouse died, a survivor with a dependent child at home keeps the joint return rates and standard deduction.
803|The Social Security number is the tax ID for individuals. A valid one is required for the earned income credit and for a child to count for the child tax credit.
804|An ITIN is a tax number from the IRS for someone who needs to file or be listed on a return but cannot get a Social Security number. It is for tax only: it does not allow work and does not qualify for the earned income credit.
805|An ATIN is a temporary number for a child who is being adopted and has no Social Security number yet. It allows the dependency claim and child tax credit items that accept it, but not the earned income credit.
806|An EIN is the tax ID number of a business or other entity, such as an estate or trust. It is needed to have employees and to file business returns. Applied for on Form SS-4.
807|Anyone paid to prepare federal tax returns must have a PTIN and put it on each return. It is renewed every year.
837|The starting rule of income tax: everything you receive is income unless the law specifically excludes it. That is why items like gambling winnings, found money and illegal income are taxable, while gifts and life insurance proceeds are not.
838|Taxable income is what the tax rates are applied to. Start with AGI and subtract the standard deduction or itemized deductions, and the 20% qualified business income deduction if any.
839|Earned income is pay for work. It matters because several benefits require it: IRA contributions, the earned income credit, the dependent care credit and the refundable part of the child tax credit.
840|Unearned income comes from owning something or from a benefit, not from working: interest, dividends, capital gains, pensions. It matters for the kiddie tax, the dependent's standard deduction, and the 3.8% net investment income tax.
841|An exclusion is income the law leaves out completely. It never appears in gross income, so it differs from a deduction, which is subtracted after income is counted. Examples: gifts, inheritances, municipal bond interest.
842|A dependent is a qualifying child or a qualifying relative. Having one opens the door to Head of Household status, the child tax credit or the $500 credit for other dependents, and other benefits.
843|A resident alien is a non-citizen who has a green card or was in the U.S. long enough to meet the substantial presence test. For tax they are treated like a citizen: worldwide income is taxed and Form 1040 is filed.
844|A nonresident alien is a non-citizen who has no green card and did not meet the substantial presence test. Only U.S.-source income is taxed, and it is reported on Form 1040-NR.
