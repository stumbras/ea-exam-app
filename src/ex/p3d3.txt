381|A lien and a levy are different steps. A lien is the government's legal claim on your property to secure the debt; you still have the property. A levy is the actual taking of property, such as money in a bank account or part of your wages.
382|Two ways to resolve a tax debt. An installment agreement pays the full amount, plus interest and penalties, over time. An offer in compromise settles the debt for less than the full amount, when the IRS doubts it can collect it all or doubts it is owed.
383|The penalty can be charged to any person who had the duty and the power to collect and pay the withheld taxes and willfully did not. Title does not decide it: what matters is who could decide which bills were paid. Willful includes paying other creditors while knowing the taxes were unpaid.
384|Trust fund taxes are amounts an employer takes from employees' pay and holds for the government: withheld income tax and the employees' share of Social Security and Medicare. The employer's own share and unemployment tax are not trust fund taxes.
385|A levy on wages keeps going every payday until the debt is paid or the levy is released. Part of each paycheck is protected, based on the standard deduction and the number of dependents the employee reports on the statement that comes with the levy.
386|A bank levy captures only what is in the account when it arrives. The bank freezes that money for 21 days before sending it to the IRS, which gives the taxpayer time to fix mistakes or make other arrangements.
387|The IRS must release a levy when the tax is paid, when the collection period has ended, when the levy creates an economic hardship (the taxpayer cannot meet basic living costs), or when an installment agreement is made that does not allow the levy to continue.
388|While an offer in compromise or an installment agreement request is being considered, the IRS generally may not levy. The protection continues for 30 days after a rejection and during any appeal of the rejection.
628|The IRS has 10 years from the date the tax was assessed to collect it. After that the debt can no longer be collected. The clock stops while certain things are pending: an offer in compromise, an installment agreement request, a collection due process hearing, bankruptcy, or the taxpayer living abroad for 6 months or more.
629|A federal tax lien comes into existence by law, with no filing, once three things have happened: the IRS assessed the tax, it sent a bill demanding payment, and the taxpayer did not pay. It attaches to everything the taxpayer owns or later acquires.
630|The Notice of Federal Tax Lien is the public filing that tells other creditors the government has a claim. The IRS must tell the taxpayer within 5 business days after filing, and the taxpayer then has 30 days to request a collection due process hearing.
631|The IRS must release a lien within 30 days after the debt is paid in full, becomes legally uncollectible, or the IRS accepts a bond guaranteeing payment.
632|Three ways to get relief from a lien without full payment. Withdrawal removes the public notice. Discharge removes the lien from one specific property so it can be sold. Subordination lets another creditor move ahead of the IRS, for example to allow refinancing.
633|A levy is the legal seizure of property to pay a tax debt. Before it, the IRS must send a bill, and then a final notice of intent to levy that explains the right to a hearing, at least 30 days before the levy.
634|The final notice of intent to levy is the last warning before the IRS takes property. From its date the taxpayer has 30 days to request a collection due process hearing with Form 12153, which stops the levy while the hearing is pending.
635|If the 30-day deadline for a collection due process hearing is missed, the taxpayer can still ask for an equivalent hearing within one year. Appeals holds a similar hearing, but collection is not automatically stopped and the result cannot be taken to Tax Court.
636|The Collection Appeals Program is a quick appeal of a wide range of collection actions: liens, levies, seizures, and denied or ended installment agreements. It is fast, but Appeals' decision is final and cannot be reviewed by a court.
637|In a few cases the IRS can levy first and offer the hearing afterward: taking a state tax refund, when collection is in jeopardy, levies on federal contractors, and certain repeated employment tax cases.
638|Through this automated program the IRS can take up to 15% of certain federal payments, including Social Security benefits, each time they are paid, until the tax debt is cleared.
639|A taxpayer's main home has special protection. The IRS can seize it only with written approval from a federal district court judge or magistrate, and never for a debt of $5,000 or less.
640|The IRS must accept an installment agreement from an individual who owes $10,000 or less in tax, will pay it within 3 years, and during the past 5 years filed and paid on time and had no installment agreement.
641|An individual who owes $50,000 or less can get a streamlined payment plan without giving detailed financial information, as long as the balance will be paid within 72 months or before the collection period ends. It can be requested online.
642|A business that still has employees and owes $25,000 or less of payroll taxes can get an express installment agreement, paying within 24 months, without a financial statement.
643|To get and keep an installment agreement the taxpayer must have filed all required returns and must file and pay on time in the future. Interest and penalties keep running, though the late payment penalty drops to 0.25% a month if the return was filed on time.
644|Form 9465 is the request for a payment plan. When financial information is needed, a collection information statement is used: Form 433-F in simpler cases, Form 433-A for individuals and Form 433-B for businesses.
645|When a taxpayer can pay something each month but not enough to clear the debt before the collection period ends, the IRS may accept a partial payment agreement. Full financial information is required, and the IRS reviews the taxpayer's finances every 2 years to see if payments can rise.
646|An offer in compromise is made on Form 656, with a financial statement on the offer version of Form 433-A or 433-B and a $205 fee. Low-income taxpayers pay no fee. An offer based on doubt that the tax is owed uses Form 656-L and requires no fee or payment.
647|There are two ways to pay an offer. Lump sum: send 20% of the offer with the application and pay the rest in 5 or fewer payments after acceptance. Periodic: send the first payment with the application and keep paying monthly while the IRS considers the offer. These payments are not refunded if the offer is rejected.
648|The IRS will not consider an offer unless the taxpayer has filed all required returns, is current with estimated tax payments and payroll tax deposits, and is not in an open bankruptcy case.
649|If the IRS does not reject an offer within 24 months after receiving it, the offer is treated as accepted. Time spent on a dispute in court over the liability does not count.
650|An accepted offer has conditions. The taxpayer must file all returns and pay all taxes on time for the next 5 years, or the full original debt can return. The IRS also keeps any refund due for tax years up to the year of acceptance.
651|When a taxpayer cannot pay anything without giving up basic living expenses, the IRS can mark the account currently not collectible and stop collecting. The debt is not forgiven: interest and penalties continue, refunds are taken, and the 10-year collection period keeps running.
652|The IRS interviews possibly responsible persons using Form 4180. It then sends Letter 1153 proposing the penalty. The person has 60 days (75 if outside the U.S.) to appeal before the penalty is assessed.
653|Income tax can be wiped out in bankruptcy only if it is old enough: the return was due more than 3 years before the bankruptcy filing, was actually filed more than 2 years before, and the tax was assessed more than 240 days before. There must be no fraud or willful evasion.
654|Filing bankruptcy automatically stops most collection, including IRS levies, while the case is open. In exchange the IRS's 10-year collection period is paused for the length of the stay plus 6 months.
655|Three kinds of relief from joint liability. Traditional relief: the other spouse's errors caused an understatement you did not know about. Separation of liability: for those divorced, separated or living apart, the tax is divided between the spouses. Equitable relief: when it would be unfair to hold you liable, including for tax shown on the return but not paid.
656|Traditional relief and separation of liability must be requested within 2 years after the IRS first takes collection action against the requesting spouse. Equitable relief has a longer period: while the IRS can still collect the tax, or within the refund period if a refund is sought.
657|After the IRS assesses a tax, it must send the taxpayer a notice stating the amount and demanding payment within 60 days. This notice is one of the steps required before a lien arises.
658|The IRS sends a series of letters before it levies. For individuals it starts with CP14, the first bill, followed by reminders, then CP504, which warns of a levy on state refunds, and finally the letter that gives collection due process rights.
963|Form 656 is the application for an offer in compromise based on doubt as to collectibility or effective tax administration. It states the amount offered and the payment terms.
964|Form 656-L is the offer used when the taxpayer truly doubts that the assessed tax is correct. No application fee, payment or financial statement is required.
965|Form 433-A is the detailed financial statement for individuals and the self-employed. It lists assets, income, expenses and debts so the IRS can decide how much the person can pay.
966|Form 433-B is the financial statement for a business: its assets, debts, income and expenses. It is used for business payment plans and offers.
967|Form 433-F is a shorter financial statement used mostly by the IRS's automated collection sites to set up payment plans for individuals.
968|Form 12153 requests a collection due process hearing after a lien filing or a final notice of intent to levy. It must be sent within 30 days of the notice. The same form is used to ask for an equivalent hearing.
969|Form 9423 starts an appeal under the Collection Appeals Program. The taxpayer first discusses the issue with the collection manager, and then files the form within a short deadline.
970|Form 12277 asks the IRS to withdraw a filed Notice of Federal Tax Lien, which removes the public notice as if it had not been filed. The debt itself may still be owed.
971|Form 14135 asks the IRS to discharge one specific property from the lien, for example so a house can be sold with clear title and the proceeds paid to the IRS.
972|Form 14134 asks the IRS to subordinate its lien, letting another lender take priority. It is granted when that helps collect the tax, as with a refinance that frees cash to pay the IRS.
973|Form 668-A is the notice of levy sent to a third party holding the taxpayer's property, usually a bank. It reaches only what the third party holds at that moment.
974|Form 668-W is the levy on wages and salary sent to an employer. It continues until released, and includes a statement on which the employee claims the exempt amount.
975|Form 668-Y is the document the IRS records with the county or state to give public notice of its lien.
976|Form 4180 is the interview used in a trust fund recovery penalty investigation. It asks who signed checks, who decided which bills to pay, and who knew the payroll taxes were unpaid.
1012|The collection statute expiration date is the end of the 10 years the IRS has to collect an assessed tax. After that date the remaining balance can no longer be collected.
1013|Collection due process is the taxpayer's right to a hearing with the independent Appeals office after a lien notice is filed or before a levy. The result can be reviewed by the Tax Court.
1014|The Collection Appeals Program gives a quick decision by Appeals on a collection action, but the taxpayer cannot go to court if they disagree with the result.
1015|The Notice of Federal Tax Lien is recorded publicly to tell creditors that the government has a claim against the taxpayer's property. It makes borrowing or selling property harder.
1016|An offer in compromise is an agreement in which the IRS accepts less than the full amount owed. The usual reason is doubt as to collectibility: the taxpayer's assets and future income cannot cover the debt.
1017|Reasonable collection potential is the IRS's measure of what a taxpayer can pay: the equity in what they own plus their expected future income after allowed living expenses. An offer usually must be at least this amount.
1018|A partial payment installment agreement is a monthly payment plan that will not pay off the full debt before the collection period ends. What remains unpaid at that time expires.
1019|Currently not collectible is a status for accounts where collecting would leave the taxpayer unable to pay basic living expenses. Collection stops for now, but the debt remains and interest continues.
1020|The trust fund recovery penalty makes a responsible person personally liable for 100% of the withheld payroll taxes a business did not pay over. It reaches past the protection a corporation or LLC normally gives.
1021|A lien is the government's legal claim against all of a taxpayer's property, present and future, as security for an unpaid tax. It does not take the property; a levy does that.
1022|If the IRS believes it will lose the chance to collect, for example because the taxpayer is hiding assets or leaving the country, it can levy immediately without the usual 30-day notice. The taxpayer gets a hearing afterward.
