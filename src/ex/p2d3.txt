375|An employer can deduct contributions to a profit-sharing plan or SEP up to 25% of the total pay of the employees covered by the plan. Example: covered payroll of $400,000 allows up to $100,000.
376|Vesting is when employer money in a retirement plan becomes the employee's to keep. Two allowed schedules: cliff, where nothing is vested until 3 years and then 100%; or graded, 20% a year from year 2 until 100% at year 6. An employee's own deferrals are always fully theirs.
377|Form 5500, the yearly report of a benefit plan, is due by the last day of the 7th month after the plan year ends: July 31 for a calendar-year plan. Form 5558 extends it by 2½ months.
378|A plan is top-heavy when key employees, such as owners and officers, hold more than 60% of the benefits. To protect the other workers, the employer must then make a minimum contribution for them, generally 3% of pay.
379|An employer can create a new SEP or profit-sharing plan for a year as late as the due date of its tax return for that year, including extensions. So a plan for 2025 can be set up in 2026 before filing.
380|A retirement account must not be used for the owner's personal benefit now. Borrowing from your IRA, selling property to it, or pledging it for a loan is a prohibited transaction. For an IRA the result is harsh: the entire account is treated as paid out on January 1 of that year.
503|An estate files Form 1041 when it has gross income of $600 or more in the year. A trust files with any taxable income, or gross income of $600 or more. Either must file if any beneficiary is a nonresident alien.
504|Form 1041 is due on the 15th day of the 4th month after the year ends, April 15 for a calendar year. Form 7004 gives an extension of 5½ months, shorter than the 6 months for most returns.
505|An estate may pick any year-end, starting on the date of death, which can delay tax for the heirs. A trust generally must use the calendar year. A revocable trust can elect to be treated as part of the estate and share its fiscal year.
506|Estates and trusts get a small exemption in place of a standard deduction: $600 for an estate, $300 for a simple trust that must pay out all income, and $100 for a complex trust.
507|Trust and estate brackets are very narrow. The top 37% rate begins at just $15,650 of taxable income for 2025, compared with over $600,000 for an individual. That is why income is usually distributed to beneficiaries, who are taxed at their own lower rates.
508|A simple trust has three features in a given year: its terms require all income to be paid out currently, it makes no gifts to charity, and it distributes no principal. The beneficiaries are taxed on the income even if it was not actually paid.
509|A complex trust is any trust that is not simple that year: it may keep some income, pay out principal, or give to charity. A trust can be simple one year and complex the next.
510|Distributable net income is the limit that makes sure trust income is taxed only once. The trust deducts what it distributes, up to DNI, and the beneficiaries are taxed on the same amount. The income keeps its character, so tax-exempt interest stays tax-exempt to the beneficiary.
511|A trustee often does not know the year's income until after year end. The 65-day rule allows distributions made in the first 65 days of the new year to be treated as made on the last day of the prior year, moving income from the trust's high brackets to the beneficiaries.
512|If the person who created a trust keeps control over it, such as the power to revoke it or to take the income, the trust is ignored for income tax. All of its income, deductions and credits are reported on the grantor's own return. A revocable living trust is the usual example.
513|In its last year an estate or trust passes its unused tax benefits to the beneficiaries who receive the property: capital loss carryovers, net operating loss carryovers, and deductions greater than income in the final year.
514|Some income was earned by the person before death but received afterward, like a last paycheck or a traditional IRA. It gets no step-up in basis. Whoever receives it reports it, with the same character it would have had for the decedent.
515|Income in respect of a decedent can be hit by both estate tax and income tax. To ease this, the person who reports the income can deduct the part of the federal estate tax that was caused by it. It is an itemized deduction.
516|To qualify under section 501(c)(3) an organization must be organized and operated only for listed purposes such as religious, charitable, scientific, literary or educational work, and no profit may benefit private individuals.
517|A 501(c)(3) organization may never support or oppose a candidate for public office. Lobbying on legislation is allowed only if it is not a substantial part of its activities; an organization can elect a clear spending test for lobbying under section 501(h).
518|An organization applies for 501(c)(3) status with Form 1023. Small ones, with expected gross receipts of $50,000 or less and assets of $250,000 or less, may use the shorter Form 1023-EZ. Filing within 27 months of formation makes the exemption go back to the start.
519|Most exempt organizations file a yearly Form 990, and its size determines which one: the electronic postcard 990-N for gross receipts normally $50,000 or less, 990-EZ for mid-size, and the full 990 for larger ones. Private foundations always file 990-PF.
520|The Form 990 series is due on the 15th day of the 5th month after the organization's year ends: May 15 for a calendar year.
521|An exempt organization that does not file its required Form 990, 990-EZ or 990-N for 3 years in a row automatically loses its tax-exempt status. It must apply again to get it back.
522|An exempt organization pays tax on income from a business that it runs regularly and that is not substantially related to its exempt purpose. If gross income from such a business is $1,000 or more, it files Form 990-T. Needing the money for charity does not make the business related.
523|Some activities are not treated as unrelated business: those run almost entirely by volunteers, selling donated goods (a thrift shop), and activities for the convenience of members, students or employees, like a hospital cafeteria. Passive income such as dividends and interest is also excluded.
524|Farmers report on Schedule F. Because farm income varies widely, they may use income averaging on Schedule J to tax part of a good year's farm income at the rates of the 3 earlier years.
525|Most businesses can only carry a net operating loss forward. A loss from farming may be carried back 2 years to get a refund of tax paid in good years, and then forward.
526|Crop insurance is normally income when received. A cash-method farmer whose practice is to sell the crop in the year after harvest may elect to report the insurance money in that following year, matching when the crop income would have come.
527|When drought or other weather forces a farmer to sell more livestock than usual, relief is available. Gain on breeding or dairy animals can be postponed if they are replaced within the allowed period, and income from other extra sales can be moved to the next year.
896|Form 990 is the yearly information return of a tax-exempt organization. It reports revenue, expenses, programs and governance, and it is open to public inspection.
897|An exempt organization files Form 990-T to report and pay tax on unrelated business income when gross income from it is $1,000 or more.
898|Every private foundation files Form 990-PF each year, whatever its size. It reports assets, grants and the excise tax on investment income.
899|Form 1023 is the application to be recognized as a 501(c)(3) organization. It is filed electronically with the organizing documents and financial information.
900|Form 1024 is the application for exemption for organizations under other paragraphs of section 501(c), such as social clubs. Social welfare organizations under 501(c)(4) use Form 1024-A.
901|Form 5500 is the yearly report an employer files for a retirement or benefit plan, giving information on its finances, investments and operation.
902|Form 5558 asks for a single extension of 2½ months to file Form 5500.
938|A SEP is a retirement plan where the employer puts money straight into an IRA for each eligible employee. Only the employer contributes, the same percentage of pay for everyone, and the plan is simple to set up and run.
939|A SIMPLE plan is for employers with 100 or fewer employees. Employees choose to defer part of their pay, and the employer must contribute each year, either by matching up to 3% or giving 2% to all eligible employees.
940|A highly compensated employee owns more than 5% of the business or earned more than the yearly dollar limit in the prior year. Plans are tested to make sure these employees do not benefit much more than everyone else.
941|Distributable net income is the trust's or estate's income available for distribution, figured under tax rules. It is the ceiling on the entity's deduction for distributions and on the amount beneficiaries must include.
942|Unrelated business income is what an exempt organization earns from a business it runs regularly that does not further its exempt purpose, such as a museum running a public parking garage. It is taxed at corporate rates on Form 990-T.
943|A simple trust must pay out all its income every year and does nothing else with the money. A complex trust is any trust that keeps income, pays out principal or gives to charity. The label is decided year by year.
944|A grantor trust is treated for income tax as if it did not exist, because the person who created it kept powers or benefits. Its income goes on that person's own return.
945|A private foundation is a 501(c)(3) organization funded by one family or company and not broadly supported by the public. It faces stricter rules: an excise tax on investment income, required yearly payouts, and limits on dealings with insiders.
946|Vesting is the process of earning the right to keep the employer's contributions to your retirement account. Leave before you are fully vested and you lose the unvested part. Your own contributions are always 100% vested.
